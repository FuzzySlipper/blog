# Lazy Whole-Ingredient Household Food System

## Goal

Eat a broad variety of recognizable ingredients while minimizing:

* Chopping and elaborate preparation
* Spoiled produce
* Dependence on highly formulated convenience meals
* Raw-produce food-safety worries
* Daily decisions about what to cook
* Sudden, enormous increases in fiber that aggravate digestion

The default meal is:

> **Protein + cooked vegetables + starch + flavorful liquid or sauce**

Most meals can be made by putting ingredients into one pot, simmering them, and serving them over rice, noodles, potatoes, or another starch.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Frozen and canned foods count as real ingredients.**
   Plain frozen vegetables, canned beans, and canned tomatoes are nutritionally useful foods, not inferior emergency substitutes.

2. **"Preserved" does not automatically mean "preservative-heavy."**
   Freezing, drying, and commercial canning are themselves preservation methods. A can of beans or tomatoes may contain little besides the food, water, and salt.

3. **Metal cans and shelf-stable glass jars use broadly similar preservation logic.**
   Choose based on ingredients, seasoning, price, and intended use—not whether the container looks artisanal.

4. **Cooked vegetables are fully legitimate vegetables.**
   Salad and raw leafy greens are optional. Cooking can reduce some heat-sensitive vitamins, but it also improves safety, digestibility, and availability of some nutrients.

5. **Variety matters across weeks, not every individual meal.**
   Rotate vegetables, legumes, proteins, starches, and flavor bases rather than trying to build a perfect bowl every night.

6. **Tolerance outranks dietary theory.**
   A nutritious ingredient that reliably causes pain or digestive chaos is not a good household staple.

7. **The best system is one that survives a bad week.**
   Frozen and shelf-stable ingredients reduce the chance that stress, illness, or distraction ends in spoiled groceries and emergency fast food.

# Ingredients to Keep Stocked

## Frozen Vegetables

Choose mostly plain, unsauced vegetables.

* Mixed vegetables
* Broccoli
* Cauliflower
* Green beans
* Peas
* Carrots
* Corn
* Spinach
* Kale or collard greens, when tolerated
* Bell peppers and onions
* Mushrooms
* Shelled edamame
* Stir-fry or Asian vegetable blends
* Squash
* Sweet potato chunks

Frozen vegetables can usually go directly into the pot without thawing.

Cook frozen vegetables unless the package specifically identifies them as ready to eat.

## Canned and Jarred Vegetables

* Diced, crushed, or whole tomatoes
* Tomato paste
* Beans: chickpeas, black beans, kidney beans, cannellini beans, etc.
* Lentils
* Corn
* Pumpkin or squash purée
* Artichokes
* Roasted peppers
* Green chiles
* Sauerkraut or other fermented vegetables, when tolerated

### Plain Versus Marinated

Plain vegetables in water or brine are the most flexible.

Vegetables packed in oil, vinegar, herbs, or seasoning should be treated as **vegetable-plus-condiment ingredients**. They may be excellent, but they will affect the whole dish.

Examples:

* Plain canned artichokes: neutral stew ingredient
* Marinated jarred artichokes: acidic, oily, seasoned ingredient
* Refrigerated deli artichokes: perishable prepared food

## Proteins

### Nearly Effortless

* Tofu
* Silken tofu
* Eggs
* Frozen shelled edamame
* Canned beans
* Canned lentils
* Dry red lentils
* Split peas
* Canned fish
* Frozen plain fish fillets
* Frozen shrimp
* Nuts, seeds, peanut butter, or tahini

### Easy Meat Options

* Rotisserie chicken, portioned and frozen
* Precooked chicken
* Frozen meatballs
* Chicken sausage
* Leftover cooked meat
* Meat cut into the pot with kitchen shears

Processed meats such as sausage, bacon, hot dogs, and cured deli meats are better as occasional flavoring or backup proteins than as the household default.

## Starches

* Rice
* Brown rice
* Barley
* Oats
* Pasta
* Quick noodles
* Rice noodles
* Potatoes
* Sweet potatoes
* Couscous
* Bulgur
* Tortillas
* Bread
* Microwave rice for especially low-energy days

Cook rice and noodles separately when preparing several portions. Otherwise, they continue absorbing broth and become swollen and mushy in storage.

## Durable Fresh Produce

* Bananas
* Apples
* Pears
* Oranges and mandarins
* Carrots
* Cabbage
* Potatoes
* Sweet potatoes
* Onions
* Garlic
* Winter squash
* Lemons or limes

Peelable fruit is useful because it requires little planning or preparation.

Wash produce before cutting it, including produce with an inedible rind, because a knife can transfer contamination from the exterior to the interior.

## Flavor Bases

Keep only a few open at once.

* Miso
* Doenjang
* Curry paste
* Curry powder
* Harissa
* Gochujang
* Jarred salsa
* Bouillon or broth concentrate
* Canned tomatoes
* Soy sauce
* Fish sauce
* Peanut butter
* Tahini
* Coconut milk
* Vinegar
* Bottled lemon or lime juice
* Sesame oil
* Olive oil
* Garlic and ginger paste
* Spice blends

Avoid combining several salty ingredients without tasting. Bouillon, miso, soy sauce, curry paste, sausage, and dumplings can produce a great deal of sodium when stacked together.

# Basic One-Pot Formula

For approximately three or four servings:

* 3–4 cups water, broth, tomatoes, or diluted coconut milk
* One 12–16 ounce bag of frozen vegetables
* One protein
* One principal flavor base
* Optional thickener or richness
* Rice, noodles, potatoes, or another starch

### Optional Thickeners

* Red lentils
* Mashed beans
* Peanut butter
* Tahini
* Coconut milk
* Tomato paste
* Cornstarch slurry
* Pumpkin purée

### Basic Method

1. Put the liquid and flavor base into a pot.
2. Add ingredients needing longer cooking, such as lentils, potatoes, carrots, or raw meat.
3. Simmer until nearly cooked.
4. Add frozen vegetables and quick proteins such as tofu, beans, edamame, eggs, or precooked meat.
5. Cook until everything is hot and safely cooked.
6. Taste and adjust salt, acidity, fat, or spice.
7. Serve over a starch.

# Vague Meal Templates

## Miso Tofu Bowl

* Water
* Miso
* Frozen mushrooms, spinach, or mixed vegetables
* Tofu
* Edamame
* Rice or noodles

Add miso near the end and avoid aggressively boiling it for a long period.

## Tomato Lentil Stew

* Canned tomatoes
* Water or broth
* Red lentils
* Frozen spinach, carrots, or mixed vegetables
* Curry powder, cumin, Italian herbs, or smoked paprika
* Rice or bread

Red lentils cook quickly and thicken the stew automatically.

## Lazy Curry

* Curry paste or curry powder
* Water, canned tomatoes, or coconut milk
* Frozen cauliflower, green beans, peppers, or mixed vegetables
* Tofu, chickpeas, chicken, or shrimp
* Rice

Coconut milk is optional. Tomato, broth, peanut butter, yogurt added at serving, or blended beans can provide body instead.

## Salsa and Bean Pot

* Jarred salsa
* Water or broth
* Black beans or pinto beans
* Frozen corn and peppers
* Optional pumpkin purée
* Rice or tortillas
* Optional egg, cheese, avocado, or yogurt

## Peanut-Tomato Stew

* Canned tomatoes
* Water
* Peanut butter
* Beans, lentils, tofu, or chicken
* Frozen spinach
* Sweet potato or carrots
* Rice

Add water gradually because peanut butter thickens considerably.

## Lazy Hotpot

* Water and bouillon, miso, or soup base
* Frozen vegetable blend
* Tofu
* Frozen dumplings
* Edamame or egg
* Noodles

Dumplings often contain less protein than expected, so tofu, egg, or edamame can make the meal more substantial.

## White Bean and Artichoke Stew

* Broth
* Cannellini beans
* Plain canned artichokes
* Frozen spinach
* Garlic
* Lemon juice or vinegar
* Olive oil
* Bread, rice, or pasta

Marinated artichokes can be used, but reduce other oil, salt, and acidity until the dish is tasted.

## Egg-Drop Vegetable Noodles

* Broth
* Frozen peas, carrots, spinach, or mushrooms
* Soy sauce or miso
* Eggs stirred into the simmering broth
* Noodles
* Sesame oil at the end

## Fish and Vegetable Pot

* Broth, tomatoes, or coconut curry liquid
* Frozen vegetables
* Plain frozen fish
* Potatoes or rice
* Herbs, curry paste, or lemon

Fish generally needs less cooking than potatoes and dense vegetables, so add it later.

## Emergency Minimum Dinner

* Microwave rice
* Frozen vegetables
* Tofu, egg, canned beans, or canned fish
* Miso, salsa, curry paste, or bottled sauce

This is still a legitimate whole-ingredient meal.

# Selecting Ingredients

## Prefer

* Short, understandable ingredient lists
* Plain frozen vegetables without heavy sauces
* Canned beans with beans, water, and salt
* No-salt-added or lower-sodium products when convenient
* Canned tomatoes with minimal additions
* Recognizable proteins
* Flavor concentrates used to season otherwise plain ingredients
* Packaging that is intact and properly sealed

## Limit as Everyday Defaults

* Complete frozen dinners
* Breaded freezer appetizers
* Sweetened canned fruit in heavy syrup
* Highly processed meat
* Products where most calories come from added sugar or refined fat
* Foods with elaborate formulations when a simpler equivalent exists

These foods do not need to be forbidden. The goal is simply to prevent them from becoming the structural foundation of the diet.

## Frozen Package Checks

Choose bags that are:

* Intact
* Fully frozen
* Not leaking
* Not heavily frosted
* Not obviously thawed and refrozen into one solid mass

Some clumping is a quality issue rather than proof of danger.

## Can and Jar Checks

Avoid:

* Bulging cans
* Leaks
* Severe rust
* Deep dents along seams
* Cracked jars
* Loose jar lids
* Jar lids with raised centers
* Contents that spurt, foam, smell abnormal, or appear pressurized when opened

Store shelf-stable cans and jars in a cool, dry, dark place.

After opening, refrigerate the contents and treat them as leftovers.

# Digestive-Tolerance Guidelines

Because both household members have ongoing intestinal symptoms:

* Increase fiber gradually.
* Avoid introducing several high-fiber foods at once.
* Begin with modest portions of beans and lentils.
* Cook vegetables thoroughly when that improves tolerance.
* Keep notes only when testing a specific suspected food.
* Do not interpret every bad digestive day as proof that the previous meal caused it.
* Favor foods that reliably work, even when they are not theoretically optimal.
* Soluble-fiber foods such as oats, barley, psilium, and some cooked legumes may be easier than large amounts of coarse bran or raw greens.
* Maintain adequate fluid intake as fiber increases.

A meal containing beans, lentils, cabbage, broccoli, onions, and bran all at once is not required to prove commitment to health.

# Storage and Meal Prep

* Prepare roughly three or four servings rather than a week-long vat.
* Eat one serving immediately.
* Refrigerate one or two servings.
* Freeze the remaining portions promptly.
* Store starch separately from wet meals.
* Freeze cooked rice in individual portions.
* Label freezer containers with the contents and date.
* Rotate frozen meals so several different options accumulate.
* Use shallow containers so hot food cools more quickly.
* Do not leave large pots sitting at room temperature for extended periods.

A useful rhythm is to cook once or twice per week and freeze part of each batch.

---

# Loose Weekly Coverage Goal

Over an ordinary week, try to include:

* Several different vegetables
* At least two kinds of fruit
* Beans or lentils
* Tofu, eggs, fish, or another protein
* More than one starch or grain
* Nuts, seeds, peanut butter, or tahini
* Some green vegetables
* Some red, orange, or yellow vegetables

This is a direction, not a scorecard.

---

# Household Standard

> Most meals should be made from recognizable ingredients, but frozen, canned, jarred, and microwave-assisted ingredients count. Dinner should usually contain a protein, cooked plant matter, a starch, and enough flavor or fat to be satisfying. Variety is pursued over time. Digestive tolerance, low waste, convenience, and continued enjoyment are part of eating well.
